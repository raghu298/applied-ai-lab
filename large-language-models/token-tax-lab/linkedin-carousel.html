Field notes · 01@raghunath

Background — what you actually pay for

LLMs don't see words. They see tokens.

Every API bill is per token. Every context window is measured in tokens. Every decode step produces one token.

So if a tokenizer chops your language into more pieces, you pay more money, fit less conversation, and wait longer — for the same meaning.

The question

Is that tax real, and how big is it? One way to find out: measure it.

Field notes · 05@raghunath

Why the tax existed — and shrank

A tokenizer only compresses what it has seen.

Tokenizers learn their vocabulary from training data. gpt2's data was overwhelmingly English, so Devanagari and Tamil got no vocabulary slots at all — just the byte fallback.

Each generation trained on more multilingual text with a bigger budget: 50k → 100k → 200k vocabulary slots. Real subwords for real scripts.

Field notes · 06@raghunath

Why it still matters

AThe bill is per token

A tax of 3× means the same chatbot conversation costs 3× more for that user's language — silently.

BContext shrinks too

A "128k context" window holds far fewer Tamil words than English words if the tokenizer is inefficient.

CLatency scales with tokens

Every extra token is another decode step. More pieces, slower replies.

DOpen models lag

Frontier tokenizers have nearly closed the gap. Many open-weight models still ship tokenizers that haven't.

Field notes · 07@raghunath

What I took away

Tokenization is language policy, written in code.

Whose language compresses well is decided by training data and vocabulary budgets — long before anyone types a prompt. The gap is closing. It closed because someone measured it.
